What changed

Changes made to the port after design reviews with the original author of the Java program, most recent first.

Seventh round

  • Saving no longer makes the document browser blink. Every save re-read the whole folder: the grid was emptied, the folder enumerated again, and every thumbnail in it decoded afresh — fifty documents meant fifty file reads and fifty tiles thrown away to put back forty-nine identical ones, which is what the flicker was. Saving over a document that is already listed now re-reads that one document and repaints its tile in place: one file read, no tiles replaced at all. A document saved under a new name is appended, at the end, and scrolled to — on purpose, rather than filed into the sorted order where it might land off-screen: a file you have just made should be where you are looking. The grid sorts itself afresh on the next refresh, the header button that used to be called reload. Only a save into a different folder redraws the lot, because then the lot really is different.
  • New and the plane editor are windows now. They were the last two <dialog> elements in the app, which put them in the browser's own top layer: they could not be moved aside to look at the figure behind them, they could not be resized, they forgot their size and place between openings, and once dismissed there was nothing anywhere to bring them back. Both are internal windows like every other panel — draggable, resizable, remembered, and listed in the windows menu as New and Plane set. The plane editor in particular is better for it: its preview can now sit beside the solid it is describing rather than on top of it.
  • Unsaved work is not thrown away without asking. Editing a figure and then opening another document, or starting a new one from a solid, used to discard the first silently — and so did closing the tab or following a link out of the app. Each of those now asks first, naming both the document you would lose and what is about to replace it, and declining leaves everything exactly where it was. The page's own exit is the browser's standard "leave site?" prompt, which is all a page is allowed to raise there.

    Only work is counted: the cells, the colors, the symmetries, the depth, and a hand-built plane sheet. The camera is not, and neither are the view switches like facet opacity or edge width — they are saved with the document, but they are a keystroke to redo, and a confirm box that fires because a slider moved is one people learn to click through. Saving, or opening a document, makes it quiet again.

  • Opening a document changes nothing until the figure is ready. A document's look — its colors, its opacity, its edge styles — used to be applied the moment the file was read, which meant it landed on the figure still on screen. Open a document built on a 120-plane solid and the OLD solid restyled itself instantly, then sat there wearing the new document's colors for as long as the arrangement took, seconds on a deep one, before finally becoming the right figure. The two halves are separated now: only what the calculation depends on is set beforehand, and everything visible waits until the new figure is on screen to receive it. The picture you are looking at simply stays as it was until it is replaced by the right one.

    While an arrangement is being intersected the status bar counts the planes as it always did, and now offers a Stop beside the count. Stopping ends the calculation the only way a long synchronous run in a worker can be ended — the worker is discarded and a fresh one started — so the half-built arrangement goes with it and the app says so rather than pretending the old figure is still there. Choosing a solid again picks up immediately.

  • The Files row keeps its buttons the size of their words. Every button in that panel stretched to fill the width, so a row that wrapped left one or two buttons sprawling across the whole panel, looking like a different kind of control from the line above; the two export buttons did the same on their own row. They now take the width their text asks for. The export pair also sat flush against the buttons above it, with no gap at all: the rule that spaces one row from the next matches only adjacent siblings, and the hidden file picker sat between the two rows, so it never applied. All seven verbs are one row now and wrap as one group, which is what they always looked like they were. Wrapped lines also get a little more air between them than the buttons have between each other, so a second line can never read as sitting on the first. Save As and Save New gained the ellipsis their neighbors carry, both opening a dialog. Presets… has left the row — it is one click away in the windows menu, where every panel lives — and so has the note about the preset shape.
  • No green outline where there is nothing to add. Holding shift over the outermost facets outlined them green, and green means "this click adds a cell" — but those facets have nothing further out, so the click could only report failure. The outline was dimmed there rather than dropped, on the reasoning that you still want to see which face you are pointing at; a faint green outline is still a green outline, and it still makes the promise. Now a gesture with nothing to act on draws no outline at all, and the status line beside it says why. A bare hover with no modifier still outlines neutrally, because pointing at a face is not a promise about a click.
  • Any group of facets can take a color of its own — and an opacity. Every coloring the app offers is a function of a group: which shell, which face class, which coset, which orbit. Edit colors…, beside the face-color menu, opens a panel listing those groups one row each, and any of them can be given a color the palette would not have chosen — and an opacity of its own. Turn one coset to glass and the cells behind it show through; turn it all the way down and its fill is gone while its edges stay, so the group becomes a wireframe of itself and the figure keeps its shape as you look past it. The colors follow the figure everywhere it goes: the 3-D view, the stellation diagram, the diagram's SVG export and every mesh format that carries color. The facet opacity slider is unchanged and now reads as what it always was — a modifier over the lot. A document saves its palette as a flat array of hex numbers, one per group with its alpha, so it reopens wearing its own colors and can be lifted whole into another figure through the panel's copy box; positions are group numbers, so a palette copied from a figure with ten cosets still lands correctly on one with five. Nothing is saved unless it differs from the palette, so documents that never touched color read exactly as before.
  • Spin is the Animation panel's job now. The spin the solid checkbox in View has gone: the Animation panel below it does the same thing and more — an axis in the figure's own frame, a rate in turns per second either way round, and a recording of exactly one turn. Two controls for one motion was one too many. Documents no longer carry the flag; older ones that do are simply read past.
  • A coloring tutorial, with every picture one click away. A new Coloring by symmetry page walks the four colorings — cosets, subgroup orbits, blends, mirror splits — through twelve classical pictures on the icosahedron, the dodecahedron, the rhombic triacontahedron and the cube, from the five tetrahedra to Wenninger's five-color table to what gray means. Every example is a saved document, so each "open in the app" link arrives already built, colored and editable; all twelve are engine-verified. Linked from the front page.
  • The figure outranks the plane frame, and the mirror cut finishes the job. The last two steps of the coloring report. Per-facet coset colors are now computed in their own right rather than copied from the planes, with the plane labeling demoted to the final tie-break: where nothing contradicts it, per-facet still equals per-plane exactly, but where the figure knows better the figure wins — the classical test is the compound of five octahedra, whose five components no per-plane coloring can separate (every icosahedron plane carries a hexagram of two octahedra), and which now comes out one color per octahedron under I/T with the compound selected. And a new mode, by cosets — per facet, mirror-split, removes the last gray: a facet that grays because its own symmetry straddles cosets is cut along that symmetry's mirror lines (or fanned about its center when the symmetry is a pure rotation), and the pieces — whose stabilizers are trivial, hence inside every subgroup — take crisp coset colors. The icosahedron under Ih by cosets of I, whose per-facet mode grays 380 mirror-straddling facets, now splits every one into halves or sextants wearing the two hands in equal measure — the classical "one face, two colors, parted by a mirror". The cut follows the figure into the 3-D view, the diagram, the diagram export and the mesh exports.
  • Blends where the cosets cannot decide, and orbits where they cannot exist. The coset coloring grayed far more often than it colored — under a full symmetry like Ih almost every subgroup grays every plane, the mirrors being nowhere to put — and a research report traced exactly why and what to do. Three of its steps are now in. Where no single coset can name a piece but a small set can, the piece wears the mix of that set, averaged in Oklab: the icosahedron under Ih by cosets of T — flat gray before — now shows twenty distinct two-color pastels, one right-handed and one left-handed tetrahedron each, both compounds of five legible on one figure; a mix that would need every color stays honestly gray. A new family by subgroup orbits — per plane, per facet, per cell colors pieces by whether some motion of the subgroup carries one onto the other; it exists for every subgroup with no gray at all, and its small classes are exactly the sub-figures the subgroup holds — T on the icosahedron's twenty planes gives 4+4+12, the two invariant tetrahedra picked out against the rest. Per cell is the piece a model painter actually holds. And two defects went: the per-facet fallback now labels all its orbits in one shared frame, stitched together through the cells they cap — on the gallery's compound of five tetrahedra under Ih/T, all sixty spikes are monochrome where before not one was — and it takes the same hand-steering from the selection as the per-plane path, so switching between the two modes can no longer flip a chiral compound into its mirror image. The stored subgroup lists in the symmetry data, long-stale generator residue that had misled the report's own survey, are regenerated from actual containment and tested so they cannot rot again.
  • A named view now looks down the axis of the group with the same name. The views ±i5, ±i3 and ±o2 exist to lay a symmetry axis along the line of sight, but the groups named for those axes were stored on different ones — the icosahedron has six five-fold axes, and D5(I) sat on a different one from the +i5 camera, while the icosahedral-frame 3-folds sat on the cube diagonal, which is the o3 axis. Choosing D5 and pressing +i5 therefore showed the figure from an angle unrelated to its own symmetry. Nine groups have been turned onto the axes their views use — each by conjugation with an element of its own parent, so it is the same subgroup type and still literally inside the parent — and the rule is now simply that a group tagged (O) sits on an octahedral axis and one tagged (I) on an icosahedral one, in both cases the one the identically-named view looks down. A test checks every case, and that nothing frame-tagged sits on an axis with no name.
  • The fifty-nine icosahedra, and the rules that make them fifty-nine. A page for all 59, generated from Du Val's symbols rather than built by hand — each symbol is parsed into shells, the shells into cells, and the result checked against the counts the literature records before anything is written. Seven more pages take the same twenty planes under different rules: demand that a face plane not be broken and 59 becomes 34, that an edge not be segmented and it becomes 25, or drop Miller's ban on shells separated from the core and it becomes 50, admitting figures with a shell hanging free of the middle. 84 distinct stellations in all, each one a document the app opens.
  • Stellation diagrams, on the page and out of it. Every figure in those galleries now carries its diagram beside its picture, and the gallery switches between the two. The drawing became a pure function — geometry in, SVG out, no canvas and no renderer — so the same code draws the card on the page and the file you export, and the generators can draw all 84 on the command line. A new export dialog asks what you want: which of the distinct diagrams, SVG or PNG, at a size and a weight of line you choose. Each SVG carries the document that produced it, so the picture can be rebuilt. The folder is asked for once and remembered, and everything goes into it — a single diagram as much as twenty. Where the browser cannot be given a folder, one file downloads and several come back as one zip.
  • Four kinds of ink, and a preview of the file. A stellation diagram is really four drawings laid over each other, and which you want depends entirely on what the picture is for. Each now has its own switch and weight: the diagram lines, the arrangement the figure was chosen out of; the faces fill; the facets outline, the divisions inside the figure where two chosen facets meet; and the faces outline, the edge with the figure on one side and nothing on the other — the line you would cut. A plate for reading is the first alone; a net is the last two with the arrangement gone. A page that would come out blank — a face the figure never reaches, or settings that draw nothing at all — is skipped, and the dialog says so before the button is pressed rather than writing an empty file.
  • How big, how full, and how heavy are three different questions. The export now asks them separately. Width and height are the picture's resolution in pixels — the PNG's dimensions, and the SVG's viewBox, which comes to the same thing because the viewBox is one unit per pixel. Scale is how much of that frame the figure fills: it multiplies the drawing's coordinates and nothing else, so 1 fits the figure with a small margin and anything above about 1.05 crops it. And a line weight is now an absolute width in pixels of the finished picture — a tenth of a pixel to ten pixels, the same range whatever the resolution — so a 0.7 px hairline is 0.7 px whether the plate is 500 pixels or 4000, where before it was a fraction of the frame and thickened as the frame grew. A picture that is not square fits the figure to the shorter side and centers it in both, and the previews take the picture's own proportions.
  • The top-left corner says which document you are in. Its name, and under it the three things that decide what it can be: the solid, the symmetry its planes were laid out with, and the symmetry its cells are chosen under — icosahedron · Ih · I. A document that has never been saved is named after exactly those, so it reads u28-Ih-I until you save it. The corner it took belonged to the Stellation link, which has moved to the far right past the ?; the walkthrough, the history and Brückner's plates are all one click further, on that page, so their three buttons have gone. So has the vertex-and-face count, which was a readout nothing depended on.
  • Coloring by cosets of a subgroup. A fourth face coloring: by coset. A menu that appears beside the color choice picks a subgroup of the polyhedron symmetry, and every facet is painted by which coset its plane belongs to — the classical pictures are plane-partitions: the compound of five tetrahedra IS the icosahedron's twenty face planes falling into five sets of four, and a spike is one color precisely because its whole surface lies in one set's planes. Pick coset subgroup T on the icosahedron and the compound wears its five colors, each tetrahedron one of them. The coloring belongs to the figure, not to the editing: it ignores the stellation symmetry entirely, so regrouping to carve out a single tetrahedron does not repaint what you made. A mirror-free subgroup counts its cosets among the polyhedron group's rotations, which is what the classical pictures do — cosets of T in I, on a solid whose full symmetry is Ih. The twenty planes carry the partition twice, once per hand of the compound, and the selection on screen decides which hand the colors follow. Pieces the cosets cannot name are gray rather than wrong. The coloring is offered two ways — one color per plane, where a whole face plane matches and the five tetrahedra appear, or one per facet, a single flat piece of surface at a time, which can separate things a whole plane cannot — color the icosahedron under Ih by cosets of I and the two hands of its chiral facets come out as the two colors. Where the per-plane labeling exists the facets inherit it, so the smaller pieces never contradict the larger. Both work in the 3-D view, the diagram, the diagram export and every mesh format that carries color.
  • A turntable, and a video of one turn. A new Animation panel under View spins the solid about an axis given in its own coordinates — 0 1 0 for y, or type in one of the figure's own symmetry axes — at a chosen number of turns per second, negative for the other way. The axis holds still on screen while the solid goes round it. Save to video records exactly one full turn from the current angle, as WebM or MP4 — an MP4 only where the browser can encode one, and the menu says so where it cannot. The turn is counted in frames, not clocked: sixty per second of turn, each exactly its share of the circle, so a slow machine takes longer to record but the video is perfectly even — every frame is in the file. The size is yours — SD, HD, Full HD, 4K, or a custom width and height snapped to the nearest even numbers — and the turn is rendered at that size rather than scaled up from the window, so a 4K file is genuinely 4K sharp. While it records, the view letterboxes itself to the video's shape, so what you watch is the framing the file is getting. The file is named in the panel, or after the document when the field is left empty; it goes into a folder you choose once and it remembers, like the exports — and a name already in that folder is a question, never a silent replacement, in the video, the solid export and the diagram export alike. And because a turn is a loop, the last frame meets the first and it repeats without a seam. The axis and speed are remembered; the spin itself always starts off, because a page that begins moving the moment it loads is a surprise, not a preference. The Files panel moved to the top of the column, where the way in and the way out belong.
  • The solid goes out through a dialog, and in five more formats. STL, OBJ, OFF, .stel and view PNG were five buttons that each wrote immediately, under a name nobody chose, into the downloads folder however capable the browser was. They are now one Export Solid…, which asks the three questions the diagram export asks: which format, what the file is called, and — where the browser allows it — which folder, once, remembered. Three formats join them: 3MF, which is what a modern slicer would rather have than an STL; and glTF, GLB, VRML 2 and X3D, for anything that displays models.
  • And it takes the colors and the edges with it. Every format that has somewhere to put a color now carries the one you are looking at — by shell, or by which face of the original solid a facet lies in, whichever the view is using. OFF puts it after each face, 3MF as base materials a slicer reads, glTF and GLB on the vertices, VRML and X3D in a Color node indexed per face. STL has nowhere to put it and OBJ would need a second file beside it, so those two are the shape alone, and the dialog says so rather than graying a box without a reason. And when the view is drawing the edges as cylinders rather than lines, those cylinders go into the file too, at the radius and in the color they are drawn in: if you are looking at tubes then they are part of the figure, not an annotation on it. The same for the symmetry elements and the coordinate frame — the rotation axes as their cylinders, the mirror planes as their rims, the frame as its three arrows, whichever of them the view is showing. And a save orientation switch writes the model facing the way the screen does, x right, y up, z toward you; left off, it is written in its own frame, which is what two figures have to share to be compared.
  • One row of file buttons. New… is the solid picker, where every new document starts. Files… is what Browse… was called, and it now answers "open a document" on any browser: the folder browser with previews where the File System Access API exists, and the plain file picker where it does not — before, the folder button was simply hidden there and opening lived under a different label. Then Save, Save As, and Save New, which is the Save As dialog opened on a freshly generated name instead of this document's: it writes a copy and leaves the original where it was, and from then on Save writes the copy.
  • Which diagrams to export is now asked with the diagrams. A solid has one diagram per kind of face — one for the icosahedron, three for the truncated cuboctahedron — and which of them you want is not a thing anyone can answer from the words all and the one shown, least of all under a low symmetry, where faces that look alike give genuinely different diagrams. So each is drawn, on its own card, with the checkbox that decides whether it is written. Every card is the file: the same drawing code on the same geometry with the same options, at the export's own resolution and shrunk to fit, so a hairline looks like a hairline instead of the several times heavier it looked when the preview was redrawn small. A face the figure never reaches shows its blank card and says so, rather than the button quietly counting one lower than the ticks suggest.
  • A picture no longer depends on the window it was made in. Thumbnails were cropped out of the live canvas, and the camera accounts for the aspect ratio — it must, or a narrow canvas would cut the sides off — so the same solid at the same zoom filled a different fraction of a wide window than a tall one. They are now drawn into a fixed square buffer, which makes the picture a property of the document rather than of the reader's screen. The same fault, and the same fix, for exported diagram PNGs.

Sixth round

  • The panels can float as windows. A toggle in the header switches the app between the docked three-pane layout and a desktop of movable, resizable windows — the solid, the diagram, the cells table and the settings each in one. Docked stays the default; a phone is always docked, floating panels there being a worse kind of scrolling. The trick underneath is that the windows adopt the existing panels rather than rebuild them: the live DOM is moved into the windows and back, so every control, every wired handler and the live WebGL context come along untouched. Window positions and which windows are open persist between visits.
  • The solid can be made translucent. A facet opacity slider in the View panel takes the facets from solid down to nothing. Below 100% the solid turns to glass and its hidden edges come through with it, so the cells inside — the one part of a stellation you can never otherwise look at — are visible through the ones in front. At 0% only the edges are left, which is a wireframe of the whole arrangement. The glass composites in true depth order, using the sorting algorithm from the original Java applet: a stellation is cut from a fixed set of planes, and those planes order the facets exactly, where a general depth sort would get these meshes wrong. Everything opaque — the edges, the symmetry axes, the mirror rims — draws first and writes real depth, and the glass then composites over it: an edge or axis deep inside is dimmed by every layer of glass in front of it, anything it hides stays hidden, and the front surface stays crisp. So the solid reads as a translucent object rather than an x-ray. The setting is saved with the document, so a figure can be kept the way it reads best.
  • The missing symmetries. The icosahedron offered thirteen stellation symmetries where it has twenty-two. The absentees were real subgroups stored about the wrong axis — D3d, the symmetry of a triangular antiprism, sits inside an icosahedron, but the stored D3d has its 3-fold axis where a textbook puts it and the icosahedron's lie along body diagonals, so the program could not see that one was inside the other. Twelve groups have been added in the frames that fit, and every symmetry the catalog uses now offers its complete subgroup lattice: Ih 13 → 23, Oh 25 types all reachable, and the same for I, O and Td. A test enumerates each lattice and fails if anything is unreachable again.
  • A third coloring: by stellation face class. Coloring by face class asks which faces are the same kind of face of the solid you started from — a property of that solid, so an icosahedron is one color however you stellate it. The new setting asks the other question: which faces the symmetry you are building under can still carry onto one another. The two agree at full symmetry and part company below it, so dropping the icosahedron to a subgroup splits its twenty faces into two colors under Th, and into twenty under E. Switching symmetry recolors immediately, without rebuilding.
  • A gallery of examples. A new Examples page: a card per stellation, and clicking one opens the app with that arrangement built and those cells chosen. It reads a catalog that keeps the curation — tags, notes, where a figure comes from — outside the documents themselves, so a document stays a thing to open rather than an opinion about which gallery it belongs to. Links name the document rather than describing it, which is what lets an example built from a hand-made plane set be linked at all.
  • One rule for a document's name. Save As offers the document's own name — the file it came from, or the preset it came from — and otherwise a generated one, now beginning stel-. Picking a solid from the catalog starts a new document, so it no longer arrives wearing the last one's name, and saving onto a name that already exists asks first.
  • Coordinate axes, and a home button that tours. The View panel can draw the x, y and z axes through the origin — red, green and blue arrows spanning the solid, each tipped with a 60° cone, at whatever thickness reads best. They show the frame the symmetry groups are written in: in the +z view x points right, y up, z toward you, and they are sized to the selection you have rather than to everything the planes could build. Beside the home button there is now a menu of the directions a solid can be seen from square-on: the coordinate axes ±x, ±y, ±z, and the symmetry axes themselves — ±i2, ±i3 and ±i5 for an icosahedron's two-, three- and five-fold axes, ±o2, ±o3 and ±o4 for a cube's. It names the one you are in, blanks itself when you turn the solid off it, and home puts you back on it: the button restores your orientation rather than moving you to a new one, so it can always undo a drag. Orientation only — your zoom stays where you left it. The isometric views are the true ones, the body diagonal landing exactly on the line of sight so the three axes leave the origin 120° apart and equally foreshortened; and the symmetry views are where a solid looks like what it is — seen down its five-fold axis, an icosahedral stellation is exactly five-fold on the screen. Each view is the shortest turn from the +z frame to its direction and nothing more, so none of them spins the model on the way: reaching the (0, 1, 1) view is a 45° tip about x, which leaves x lying across the screen where it began. The isometrics keep their own roll, the one that stands y upright — that pose is what an isometric view is for.
  • Plane sets are stored as data, not as text. A document's custom plane set used to be saved as the lines of the editor — nx, ny, nz, d and a group name, re-parsed on every read. It is now a list of proper JSON objects with a normal, a distance, a symmetry and a factor, each checked when it is read and when it is written, naming the row at fault. A mistyped plane set now refuses to open instead of quietly building a different solid, and numbers keep their full precision because they are never turned back into text. Documents saved in the old form still open: their lines are read once and re-saved as data.
  • One New dialog, and planes from anywhere. The polyhedron picker is now the single front door: New starts from a polyhedron, a preset, a file on your computer, or your own plane set, and hands off to whichever of those you choose. The plane editor imports from the same three sources — a catalog solid, a preset, or a document from disk, each reduced to one row per symmetry class — so a plane set can be assembled out of anything the program can already open. Each row also gains a factor that scales its distance from the origin, which slides the plane along its own normal: the same solid's planes can be imported twice and set at two depths without retyping a single number.
  • Four more of Brückner's models, rebuilt. The Brückner page gains the compound of five cubes (Taf. XII, 24) — computed to be exactly the first five shells of the rhombic triacontahedron — the other three regular star polyhedra, which are the dodecahedron's complete set of stellations (drawings Taf. VII, 21–23; models Taf. IX, 7 and 16 and Taf. X, 5), and Kepler's stella octangula (Taf. VII, 20), the octahedron's one and only stellation. Every entry quotes the passage of the book that places the model on its plate.
  • Make planes, made friendly. The plane editor is rows instead of a raw sheet: each row holds one plane as four copy-and-pasteable numbers (normal and distance, full double precision), a symmetry group of its own, and the computed orbit size. Groups mix freely — icosahedron planes beside cube planes in one arrangement. A live 3D preview shows every plane as a spoke from the origin with a disc lying in it, colored by row, and an import menu appends any catalog solid's planes reduced to one row per symmetry class — the icosahedron imports as a single Ih row of orbit twenty. Documents are unchanged: the rows are the same plane sheet they always saved.
  • The stellation symmetry no longer clears your cells. Changing it used to reset the selection to the core and wipe the undo history. Now the selection, the history and the camera all stay put, and the symmetry means what it should have meant all along: how big a bite the next click takes. Every editing operation toggles the whole orbit of the clicked cell under the current group — so you can rough out a stellation under the full symmetry, drop to a subgroup (E takes single cells) for the asymmetric touches, and come back, without losing a thing. A box in the Cells table holding only some of its pieces shows a faint fill, says "k of n pieces" on hover, and fills whole on a click. Documents whose selection is whole orbits save exactly as before; one that is not saves in a new notation an old build declines to guess at, and exports to .stel under E, which the original Java program reads exactly.
  • First visits no longer open on a wireframe. A first visit — any browser with nothing remembered yet — read the absent opacity setting as 0% and booted into an invisible wireframe: the blank 3D view reported on Android. And on a phone the two views tiled the whole screen while swallowing every touch gesture, so the page could not be scrolled at all. The views now leave the statusbar and the top of the settings panel showing, which gives the page a place to scroll from.
  • Symmetry elements, smoother and adjustable. The mirror-plane rims and fills are drawn at four times the segments, so they read as circles rather than polygons, and a thickness slider under the symmetry checkboxes scales the axis tubes and rims from hairline to emphatic — the reach of each element stays put, only the girth changes.
  • Edges can be thin cylinders. Each kind of edge — face and facet, independently — can be drawn as slender capped cylinders instead of flat lines: real geometry with thickness in the model, shaded by the same lights as the solid, so an edge catches a highlight and shades round. The choice is per kind because the two read differently: the solid's own edges carry a cylinder well, while the hairline facet subdivision can shimmer as sub-pixel geometry and often stays cleaner as lines. The width sliders now move in steps of 0.1, down to 0.1; being real geometry, cylinders grow as you zoom in where the flat lines keep a constant screen width. Saved with the document like the rest of the edge styling.
  • One menu for every window. The windows menu in the header lists them all — the four panels, the presets and the document browser — with a tick beside the ones that are open, and it opens and closes any of them. It is there in both layouts, because the two browsers float over the docked layout just the same. Closing a window with its ✕ therefore always has a way back.
  • Presets with pictures, instead of four buttons. The Presets… button opens a window of thumbnails for ready-made stellations the site ships; picking one loads it. The four numbered sample buttons are gone, converted into the first four presets. They are ordinary .json files with a .json.png thumbnail beside each, served as plain images, so the browser does all the work and this runs everywhere, phones included.
  • Documents can live in a folder, not just downloads. In browsers that allow it (Chrome, Edge — the File System Access API), Browse… opens a thumbnail view of a folder you choose, Save overwrites the file a document came from and refreshes its preview, and Save As… writes a json + thumbnail pair into a folder you pick. The folder is remembered, so later visits need only a one-click permission, not the picker again. Elsewhere — Firefox, Safari, mobile — those buttons simply do not appear, and Save stays the .json download it always was. Nothing breaks on any browser.
  • The narrow-screen layout works now. The responsive rules had been dead for a while — a stylesheet ordering bug meant the two views never stacked on a small screen. They do again: below a certain width the views stack, then the side panel drops below them, then on a phone the whole thing is one scrolling column. The window chrome is built on pointer events, so it drags with a finger; the file and preset windows become near-fullscreen sheets where a floating window would be pointless.

Fifth round

  • Facets can be colored by which face of the original solid they lie in. The 3D view had one coloring, a hue per shell outward from the core. A second is now offered in the View panel: a hue per symmetry class of original face, so every facet lying in the same kind of face matches however far apart they are, with undersides in the same hue darkened. An icosahedron has one kind of face and so takes two colors; an icosidodecahedron has two and takes four. The classes are orbits of the face planes under the polyhedron's group, not the stellation group chosen below it — the kind of face is a property of the solid you started from. The diagram follows the same choice: it is drawn on one plane, so it takes that plane's color and the only variation left is the above/below split it already draws.
  • Face edges and facet edges are drawn separately. Every edge of every facet used to be drawn in one color and one weight, which buries the shape of the solid in the arrangement that produced it. Where two facets of different planes meet there is a real crease, an edge of the solid; where two facets of the same plane meet, the surface runs flat across the join and the line only records how the arrangement was cut up. Each kind now has its own toggle, color and width. How much this matters depends on the selection: whole consecutive shells have no facet edges at all, their per-plane regions being disjoint, while the shipped samples are close to half and half — 240 of each for sample_02.
  • The solid follows a virtual pointer on a spring. A mouse reports in uneven jumps and applying each one directly makes the model stutter, so the trackball now drives a unit mass pulled by a spring whose far end is the real pointer, damped by fluid friction — the AnimatedPointer from the author's SymmHub, with its physics unchanged. Releasing the button switches the spring off and the mass keeps its speed, so a throw spins as fast as it was thrown, with no cap. This replaced three separate mechanisms whose release behavior split at a threshold: a throw twice as hard used to produce exactly the same spin as one just over the line. A drag that decelerates before release still stops dead, which is the fault the Java drag fix diagnosed.
  • A parallel projection, so shape no longer depends on distance. Selecting a cell in the outermost layer turned the view inside out: the fitted camera sat inside the selected cell and 20 of its 152 vertices fell at or behind the eye point. A parallel projection has no eye to fall behind, so the failure cannot recur at any depth — and it settles the three framing problems logged on 6 August, which were one problem, since a camera that moves changes the perspective of everything that did not change.
  • The tutorial figures re-fit only when the frame no longer suits. Following the content on every change meant that switching one outer cell resized the whole model, and a jump that large reads as the model changing rather than the selection. The frame is now left alone while the content sits comfortably inside it, and eases to a new fit only when the model would be cut off or has shrunk to a speck.
  • A capped arrangement no longer builds shells it cannot support. The snub dodecahedron came apart past layer 11 — cells scattered through the outer shells instead of tiling them. Not precision: the facet cap was dropping facets whose split would carry them past it, so at layer 12 only 150 cells of 1650 survived. Comparing capped against uncapped builds showed the first differing layer is always the cap itself and everything below identical, so the build now stops there: fewer layers, all of them true. Raising the depth control returns the rest.
  • The site has a front door. Arriving at the project put a three-pane workspace in front of you, with the documentation reachable only from a header link inside it. The app is now stellation_app.html and the address you land on is a short description with a card for each page. Links shared before the move still work — the app keeps its whole state in the URL hash, and the landing page forwards a hash that looks like app state.
  • The JavaScript is in three trees rather than one. docs/app/js is the application, docs/lib the layer it shares with the tutorial and sample pages, docs/js those pages' own scripts. Imports go through barrels that list every name explicitly, so a future move is one file to edit rather than every consumer, and a collision between two shared modules is an error at load instead of a binding that quietly becomes undefined.

Fourth round

  • The camera never moves within an arrangement. The third-round fix pinned foreshortening to the selection's bounding sphere, but the camera still traveled as that sphere changed — so toggling an outer cell subtly changed the perspective of everything else, on both machines this time. Distance is now set once per build, from the radius of the whole buildable arrangement; selecting and deselecting cells changes it by exactly nothing, and only zoom — a flat projection scale — does the framing.
  • Each view now works at its own scale. The solid adds or removes one cell: shift the cell sitting on the clicked face, ctrl the one behind it, green and red as before. The diagram toggles the cell on either side of its plane: shift the one beneath, ctrl the one on top — in gold and blue, because those are toggles and green/red would lie. The group operation — a box together with its whole supporting set — lives only in the Cells table, on either modifier, and it too is a toggle. The previous "same gestures everywhere" was this port's invention, not the design.
  • The Cells table counts again. Every plain box drew the digit 0 — it was showing its sub-cell index, which is always zero for a box with one sub-cell, instead of its cell index. Visible the moment a low symmetry put several cells in one row.
  • Du Val's letters, where they exist. For the icosahedron under Ih the boxes now read a, b, c, d, e₁, e₂, f₂, f₁, g₁, g₂, h rather than positions in a sorted list. The identification of which orbit is which letter is the derivation in the research notes, the same one the walkthrough's Ef₁ preset was verified against. Every other solid keeps indices — no other solid has an accepted lettering.
  • Scrollbars in the Cells table, horizontal and vertical, drawn when the table overflows and draggable. And switching to a smaller table resets the scroll, so it no longer comes back cropped to where a bigger table left it.
  • Symmetry elements in class colors, with a legend. Inequivalent elements — axes no operation of the group carries onto each other — get different colors; for Ih the legend reads C₅ ×6, C₃ ×10, C₂ ×15, m ×15, S₁₀ ×6, S₆ ×10, which is the textbook census. Mirror planes are rimmed with thin tori of the same thickness as the axis cylinders, so an edge-on plane reads as a bar instead of vanishing; the faint translucent fill stays, having been looked at and kept. Checkboxes gray out when the group has none of that kind.
  • The elements are marked on the diagram too. A dot where each rotation axis pierces the drawing plane, a dashed line where each mirror plane crosses it, in the same class colors — what the Java applet's diagram checkboxes drew. One toggle: mark on the diagram.
  • A real flick keeps the solid spinning. Release while moving fast enough and it spins on at that rate until you catch it — the Java behavior. A gentle release still just coasts to a stop.
  • Make planes, ported. The original program's plane editor: one plane per line — nx ny nz d and optionally a symmetry group to multiply that plane by — seeded from the current solid's own planes, built into an arrangement like any catalog solid, saved into the JSON document. One line, 0 0 1 1 Ih, builds the rhombic triacontahedron's thirty-plane arrangement.
  • Winding repair moved into the engine. The face-orientation repair the derivation tool needed lives in core.js now (orientFaces), because it is a property of the catalog data, not of one tool: most solids in the file are not consistently wound, and anything that ever culls back faces or integrates a volume will need it.
  • Crowding relief for the zoomed-out diagram. Far below 1× the facet strokes thin and fade with the zoom, so the bundle of near-parallel traces shimmers less. This is mitigation, not elimination — at that scale there are more lines than pixels and no stroke width is honest.
  • Documented, not changed: the ten hemi-dual solids whose faces are approximations of faces with vertices at infinity (clamped to ~100 in the data, hence genuinely non-planar) are now documented, along with a short how-to for serving this site from GitHub Pages.

Third round

  • Resizing the 3-D pane no longer changes the perspective. The camera distance was derived from the narrower of the two field-of-view angles, so it depended on the shape of the canvas — drag the splitter and you could watch vertical lines bend. Distance now depends only on the solid's radius; a narrow canvas is framed by scaling the projection, which cannot bend a straight line. Measured: the ratio distance ÷ radius is now bit-identical from 1600×600 to 300×1200.
  • Toggling a cell no longer changes the perspective either. Same root cause seen from the other side: the radius was floored at 1, so any selection smaller than the first one sat too far from the camera and flattened. The Windows-only symptom was the Cells panel gaining a scrollbar and resizing the canvas. Perspective is now invariant under every selection.
  • Symmetry elements are solid geometry, so the solid hides what is behind them. Axes are drawn as cylinders into the same depth buffer as the stellation instead of being painted over the top. Three separate toggles: rotation axes, mirror planes, and rotoreflection (Sn) axes — the three-dimensional cousin of a glide reflection. Mirror planes are drawn as a solid rim with only the faintest wash inside, because fifteen translucent discs compound into an opaque ball that hides the very thing they explain.
  • Turning the solid is smoothed, and a flick coasts. Pointer motion is banked and spent a fixed fraction per frame, which evens out the uneven steps a mouse reports; on release the recent speed is added to the buffer so the motion carries on and settles. A first-order filter rather than a spring, so it cannot overshoot and there is no ringing to tune out.
  • Undo and redo, as buttons beside core / + layer / all / clear and on ctrl+Z and ctrl+shift+Z (cmd works too). Every change to the cell set is undoable, a hundred deep. Carving from a high cell takes its whole supporting set, which can reach the core, and there is no way to reconstruct what was there from what is left.
  • Green adds, red removes, in all three views. Holding shift outlines the target green, the carve modifier outlines it red, and the same two colors are used in the 3-D view, the diagram and the Cells table. Releasing the modifier clears the outline immediately. When the gesture would do nothing — nothing further out to add, nothing behind the face to remove — the outline is dimmed rather than dropped, so you still see where you are pointing but know the click is spent.
  • Diagram face is a dropdown of the faces that actually differ. It was a number with no upper limit, so most values did nothing. The list is now the orbits of the face planes under the chosen symmetry, each named by the polygon at the center of its diagram. The cuboctahedron under Oh offers exactly two — triangle · 8 planes and square · 6 planes — and lowering the symmetry splits them, as it should.
  • The collapse arrow points back the way it opened — ▸ to open a group of sub-cells, ◂ to close it, rather than a downward arrow that read as unrelated. The arrow and the count of hidden parts are larger, heavier and in a color you can find.
  • The view is kept in the URL and in saved documents. The hash now carries the orientation and zoom, so a reload comes back to the same angle and a link shows the recipient the same picture. Saved JSON carries them too.
  • A stale build cannot be served silently any more. A good part of the session went on a bug that had been fixed an hour earlier — the browser had an old app.js and neither ctrl+shift+R nor ctrl+F5 shifted it. The site now sends Cache-Control: no-cache for code and markup, so every visit revalidates, and the help panel prints which build you are looking at.
  • Crossed faces get the right plane. Newell's method sums a signed area, and a bow-tie face has two lobes that cancel — so its normal came out of rounding error. 413 faces across 15 solids were affected. Where the sum collapses, the plane is now fitted to the vertices instead. Seven solids whose faces are otherwise planar go from wrong to correct to machine precision; the rest are the hemi-duals, whose face data is genuinely non-planar.
  • Dropped planes are declared. When a solid's faces do not all yield a usable plane, the status line reads ⚠ 8 of 12 planes rather than simply 8 planes, and the tooltip says why. Building an arrangement out of fewer planes than the solid has is the one failure that looks perfectly healthy: you get a stellation, of a different solid.

Second round

  • The hover outline sat off the face it marked. A regression from making the model scale sticky: the highlight still computed its own scale, so the yellow triangle floated over the wrong place.
  • Fit and home are on the view itself. Fit existed but was inside a collapsed panel section, so it could not be found. Both are now always visible in the corner of the 3-D view. home is the canonical orientation — x right, y up, z toward you.
  • Zoom scales the projection instead of moving the camera, so magnifying no longer swings the viewpoint round the solid, and the range is wide enough to frame the largest arrangements. Picking was corrected to match the new frustum.
  • Fit eases into place rather than snapping, over about four tenths of a second, and home turns the solid to the canonical orientation along the shortest arc. Any drag or wheel cancels the movement, so it never fights the pointer.
  • The camera now retreats as the selection grows. Holding it at a fixed distance was wrong once a stellation reached past radius 6 while the camera sat at 3.3 — the viewpoint ended up inside the solid. It now backs off by the same factor the projection is scaled by, which leaves the on-screen size and the perspective untouched.
  • ctrl is the exact inverse of shift. Both now walk inward: shift adds a cell with everything supporting it, ctrl removes that same set. Before, shift reached inward and ctrl reached outward, so the pair never canceled.
  • Sub-cell groups start collapsed, with an arrow beside each showing how many parts are hidden. Sub-cells are drawn smaller than their parent so the grouping reads at a glance. Under a low symmetry one orbit can hold 120 sub-cells, which used to run off the panel.
  • Symmetry axes can be drawn in the 3-D view — the rotation axes of the current stellation group, through the origin. Checked against group theory: 31 for I and Ih, 13 for O and Oh, 7 for T, 3 for D₂.
  • Inward-facing regions in the diagram are less faint. They were pale enough to be missed.

Controls

The largest group, and the one that changes how the app feels.

  • Shift always adds; ctrl always removes. Neither toggles any more. Shift-clicking an already-selected cell used to undo the previous shift-click, so the same gesture sometimes added and sometimes removed depending on what you had just done. The two are now plain inverses.
  • A bare click no longer edits in the 3-D view or the diagram. It turns the solid and pans the diagram respectively; only the Cells table spends a plain click on toggling. Previously a click meant one thing in one pane and something else in another.
  • Right-click does nothing. It used to carve. On macOS ctrl-click is the system right-click, so the two are told apart by whether the ctrl key was actually held — a real right-click is ignored, a ctrl-click removes.
  • One vocabulary on both platforms. The remove key is now called ctrl everywhere, because ctrl-click now works on macOS too. Option-click still works there as an alternative.

The Cells table

  • Turned the right way up. The core is now the bottom row and each shell sits on the one below it, so the table grows the way the solid does — from the inside out. Row numbers are unchanged.

The diagram

  • Inward-facing surfaces are drawn pale and dashed. The diagram shows the surface of the solid, not its volume, and a filled region invited the reading that there is material behind it — when an inward-facing face means the opposite, that there is a cavity. Solid fill now means outward, pale means inward.

Symmetry

  • Only genuine subgroups are offered. The dropdowns listed all 85 groups regardless of the solid; for the icosahedron they now list the 13 that are actually subgroups of Ih, and the stellation symmetry is restricted to subgroups of whatever the polyhedron symmetry is set to. Membership is decided by testing the matrices, not from a hand-written table.
  • That also fixes the "everything is broken" case. The all-zeros table came from choosing T (order 12) as the polyhedron symmetry with I (order 60) as the stellation symmetry — not a lowering at all, so every group had exactly one sub-cell. That pairing can no longer be selected. The sub-cell splitting itself was correct: under Ih → T an icosahedron's layers split 3/5/10, and under Ih → E into all 120.

Layout and view

  • The panes can be dragged. Both dividers resize, and the positions are remembered — a deep arrangement can run a row of the Cells table far wider than the default column.
  • The solid no longer resizes itself as you build. The scale was recomputed on every change, so adding a shell shrank everything already on screen. It is now fixed when the arrangement is built, with a fit button to rescale on demand.
  • Edge weight now defaults to 1.

Documentation

  • A short Controls & views page — the two gestures, the three views and what each pane is for, on one screen. The Cells panel now links there instead of to the walkthrough, under a shorter label.
  • This page.

Investigated, not changed

Deliberately left alone, with the findings written down.

  • Planes through the origin. Described in the session as a fundamental defect: a plane stored as a single point cannot represent one that passes through the center, which is why twenty hemipolyhedra and their duals are commented out of the catalog — the reason is written in the source, in the author's hand.

    Investigated rather than fixed, on the instruction not to start it yet. The short version: the JavaScript port already stores four numbers, so the representation is not the blocker — one guard line is. Removing it does let all ten hemipolyhedra build, u80 included, in under a third of a second. But the guard turns out to be doing a second, unrelated job, and a prototype that removed it naively destroyed seven working solids. The discriminator that separates the two cases cleanly is face planarity, which differs between them by sixteen orders of magnitude. Written up with both prototypes, the measurements and an acceptance test in the design notes.
  • Clicking a region that has cells on both sides. On the diagram a plane has a cell above and a cell below, and there was a proposal to reach them separately — shift for one, ctrl for the other. Left as it is for now: shift and ctrl are spent on add and remove, which is the more valuable pair, and the session concluded "counterintuitive but ok, can leave as is".
  • 3-D rotation in the original Java applet. Noted as a separate to-do against the original program, not this port.

Questions still open — what the "polyhedron" symmetry is for, what "compensate completely" should mean, the cube's orientation, and the cells-versus-surface confusion — are written up in the design notes. Source and notes on GitHub. See Controls & views for how the app works now.
