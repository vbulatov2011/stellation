Controls & views

Three views of one selection, and each view works at its own scale: the solid adds and removes one cell, the diagram toggles the cell on either side of its plane, and the Cells table — only the Cells table — moves whole supporting sets.

The gestures, per view

Viewshift + clickctrl + click
solid Add the one cell sitting on the clicked face. Remove the one cell behind it.
diagram Toggle the cell beneath the plane — the one this region caps. Toggle the cell resting on it, above the plane.
Cells table Either modifier: toggle the box together with its whole supporting set — on if it was off, down to the core; off if it was on. A bare click toggles just the box.

Why the difference: in the two graphical views you are pointing at a place, and the natural unit there is the single cell on one side of it. The group operation — a cell with everything supporting it — needs the table, where the support structure is actually visible. The colors say which is which: green adds and red removes in the solid; the diagram's toggles are gold for beneath and blue for above, because there a click is a toggle, not an add or a remove.

Windows, Linux and macOS. ctrl-click works on all of them. On macOS ctrl-click is also the system's secondary click, so the page receives it as a context-menu event rather than as a click; that case is handled, and ⌥ option-click does the same job if you prefer it. A plain right-click does nothing, deliberately.

The three views

All three show one selection of cells. Editing in any of them changes the other two immediately, because there is only ever one selection.

1 · The solid

What the thing looks like.

  • drag — turn it
  • scroll — zoom
  • right-drag — pan, sliding the picture without turning it. On a touchscreen, drag with two fingers and spread them to zoom; on a trackpad, a sideways two-finger swipe does it with no button at all
  • fit — undoes both the pan and the zoom
  • home — back to the orientation chosen in the menu beside it, however far you have turned the solid since. The menu names the direction you are seeing it from and goes blank the moment you turn off one: the coordinate axes ±x, ±y, ±z, and then the symmetry axes, named for the solid and the order of the axis — i for the icosahedron, o for the cube and octahedron: ±i2 (0, 0, 1), ±i3 (0, 1/τ, τ) a face center, ±i5 (1, 0, τ) a vertex; ±o2 (0, 1, 1) an edge, ±o3 the body diagonal, ±o4 (0, 0, 1). Those are where a solid looks like what it is: seen down its five-fold axis an icosahedral stellation is exactly five-fold on the screen. Each name also names the axis the matching subgroups turn about, so choosing D5 and pressing +i5 looks straight down that group's own axis. i2 and o4 are the z axis under two other names — z is a two-fold of the icosahedron and a four-fold of the cube — which saves working that out when you want it by what it is. Each is the shortest turn from the +z frame to that direction, with no roll on top, so +o2 is a 45° tip about x and nothing else, leaving x across the screen where it started. The two o3 views are the exception, rolled so that y stands upright — the true isometric, the three axes leaving the origin 120° apart and equally foreshortened. Orientation only, so your zoom stays put
  • shift-click a face — add the cell just outside it
  • ctrl-click a face — remove the cell just inside it

You are always pointing at a face, which has a cell on each side. Shift takes the one further out, ctrl the one further in — one cell at a time, so you grow and carve exactly at the surface you can see. Hold a modifier and the outline tells you whether the click will land: on the outermost facets there is nothing further out to add, so holding shift over them draws no outline at all.

2 · The diagram

One face plane, cut by all the others.

  • drag — pan
  • scroll — zoom about the pointer
  • fit — center the diagram and reset the zoom
  • shift-click a region — toggle the cell beneath it
  • ctrl-click — toggle the cell on top of it

This is the classic stellation diagram, and it draws the surface of the solid, not its volume. A region filled solid is a face looking outward; a region filled very pale is a face looking inward, lining a cavity. Pale therefore means there is a hole behind it.

3 · The Cells table

Every cell there is, whether or not you can see it.

  • click — toggle one cell
  • click a row number — the whole layer
  • drag, wheel or the scrollbars — move about a wide table
  • ctrl-click a box — toggle it with its whole supporting set (this view only)

Rows are shells with the core at the bottom, each built on the one below. A box is a set of cells the symmetry carries onto one another; a shaded group splits into sub-cells, which is where handedness lives. The full walkthrough →

Why a plain click does nothing in two of the three. In the solid a drag turns the model and in the diagram it pans, so a bare click there is the beginning of a gesture rather than a command of its own. Only the Cells table, which never needs dragging to navigate, spends the bare click on toggling.
Floating windows. The header's windows toggle puts each panel in a window you can move and resize; drag a window by its title bar, resize it from the corner grip, close it with the ✕, and reopen a closed one from the windows menu beside the toggle. That menu lists every window there is — the presets and the document browser are in it too, so they can be opened and closed from the same place whether or not the panels are floating. Esc closes the top window. The layout persists, so the app opens the way you left it. On a phone the panels are always docked.

The panel

core · + layer · all · clear
Walk outward a shell at a time. core alone is the original polyhedron; core + one layer is its first stellation.
fit
Rescale so the current selection fills the frame. The scale is otherwise left alone while you work, so adding a shell does not shrink everything you have already built.
polyhedron / stellation symmetry
The first is the symmetry the cells are grouped under; the second, which must be a subgroup of it, is the editing symmetry: every add or remove toggles the whole orbit of the clicked cell under it. Lowering it splits each group into sub-cells — that is what lets you take one hand of a chiral pair, and at E a click takes a single cell. Changing the stellation symmetry never touches what is already selected, so you can rough out a form under the full symmetry, refine it asymmetrically under a subgroup, and switch back; a box in the Cells table then holding only some of its pieces shows a faint fill, and a click fills it. Only genuine subgroups of the solid's own point group are offered — a name in brackets, like D3d(I) or S6(O), is that group set about the parent's own axes rather than the textbook ones, which is how a triangular antiprism's symmetry can be offered inside an icosahedron at all.
face colors
Five ways to color the facets. By shell, one hue per layer outward from the core, so you can read how deep a piece sits. By face class, one hue per kind of face of the original polyhedron — a property of the solid you started from, so an icosahedron has one kind however you stellate it, with its undersides the same hue darkened. By stellation face class, the same but grouped under the stellation symmetry: the two agree at full symmetry and part company below it, because a subgroup can no longer carry every face onto every other. The icosahedron's twenty faces are one class under I, two under Th, and twenty under E — so this is the coloring that shows what the symmetry you are building under actually treats as the same. And two colorings by the cosets of a subgroup, chosen in the menu that appears below the color choice — see the next entry.
Edit colors…
Opens the Colors panel: one row for every group the reading above can name — each shell, each face class, each coset or orbit — with the group's color and its own opacity. A group at full opacity is drawn as always; below it the group turns to glass and what sits behind it shows through; at zero its fill is gone altogether and only its edges remain, which is how to look past one component of a compound at the rest without losing the shape of the thing you are looking past. Gray gets a row too, so "no coset fits" can be faded like any other group.

The colors are the figure's, not the app's: they follow it into the diagram, the diagram export and every mesh format that carries color, and a saved document reopens wearing them. The panel's copy box holds them as plain hex — the same array the document saves — so a palette can be lifted from one figure and pasted onto another; each position is a group number, so the colors land on the groups they came from even when the two figures have different numbers of them. Reset all gives the palette back. The facet opacity slider still works as it did, over the top of whatever the groups say.

coset subgroup
Shown when a coset or subgroup-orbit coloring is chosen: a subgroup of the polyhedron symmetry, deliberately independent of the stellation symmetry — the stellation symmetry is how you edit, and changing it never repaints the figure.

The two entries are the same cosets applied to different pieces — one color per plane, or one per facet. Nothing about the cosets differs between them; only which pieces share a color.

Per plane paints every facet by which coset its plane belongs to: the subgroup's own set of planes, and one translate of that set per coset. This is the classical picture and the reason the coloring exists — set the polyhedron symmetry to I and the subgroup to T, and the icosahedron's twenty face planes fall into five sets of four, each set the faces of one inscribed tetrahedron, so the compound of five tetrahedra wears five colors, one per tetrahedron. The chiral group matters: inside the full Ih every face plane is held by mirrors that T does not contain, no coset can name it, and the figure grays — honestly, rather than being given colors it has no right to.

Per facet is the same labeling applied to the smaller pieces, and it can say things whole planes cannot. Color the icosahedron under Ih by cosets of I, index two: the two hands of its chiral facets come out as the two colors, where the plane coloring can only gray, both hands lying in the same planes. Where the per-plane labeling exists the facets simply inherit it, so the two agree and the smaller pieces never contradict the larger.

Gray always means the same thing: no coset can name this piece. Either the subgroup holds it still — the core cell, fixed by everything — or its stabiliser contains symmetries the subgroup lacks, as C5 cannot name a face held by a three-fold axis.

Where no single coset can name a piece but a small set can, the piece wears the mix of that set instead of gray. The mixing is exact, not decorative: every piece of an orbit blends the same number of cosets — the translates of the reference sub-figure that contain it — and the count is forced by how the piece's own symmetry overlaps the subgroup. The icosahedron under Ih by cosets of T, formerly all gray, is the picture this exists for: each face mixes exactly one right-handed and one left-handed tetrahedron of the ten, twenty distinct pastels, both chiral compounds of five readable at once. A mix that would need every color says nothing, so those pieces stay gray.

Mirror-split — the per-facet coloring's finishing move — removes even that gray. A piece grays when its own symmetry straddles cosets; cutting it along that symmetry's own mirror lines (or fanning it about its center, when the symmetry is purely rotational) leaves pieces whose only symmetry is none at all, and those sit inside every subgroup, so every piece takes a crisp coset color at the full palette. The icosahedron under Ih by cosets of I shows the classical result: every mirror-straddling face splits into two hands' colors along its mirror line. The cut is purely a coloring device — the cells, the selection and the geometry of the figure are untouched.

By subgroup orbits — per plane, per facet or per cell — is the other half of the question. Two pieces share a color exactly when some motion of the subgroup carries one onto the other, so where the cosets ask "which copy of the sub-figure is this?", the orbits ask "what does the subgroup itself preserve?" — its invariant sub-figures appear as the small classes, T on the icosahedron's twenty planes giving 4+4+12 with the two invariant tetrahedra picked out. Orbits exist for every subgroup, need no reference choice, and never gray; the price is that the class sizes vary, because orbits are not cosets. Per cell colors whole solid pieces — the unit a physical model is painted in.

One caveat worth knowing. A coset labeling is canonical only within one orbit: swap an orbit's representative for one outside the subgroup and every label in it shifts together. The planes are a single orbit, which is what makes them a shared frame for everything else. Where no per-plane labeling exists, each facet orbit is labeled on its own, so it still separates the hands but the particular colors need not correspond from one orbit to the next.

Both colorings follow the figure into the diagram, the diagram export and every mesh format that carries color, and a saved document remembers them. The gallery's compound of five tetrahedra opens already colored this way — and the coloring tutorial walks all of these modes through twelve classical pictures, each one click from the app.

build out to N shells
How far out to carry the arrangement. Chosen per solid; raise it if shift-click reports nothing further out.
diagram face
Which face's plane the diagram is drawn on. Only faces the chosen symmetry cannot carry onto one another are listed — the rest would give the same picture.
symmetry elements
The group's rotation axes, mirror planes and rotoreflection axes, drawn as solid geometry through the origin so the stellation hides what is behind them. Inequivalent elements get different colors; the legend names each class, and mark on the diagram shows where the same elements meet the diagram's plane.
coordinate axes
The x, y and z axes through the origin, drawn as colored arrows spanning the solid — red, green and blue, each an arrow whose head is a 60° cone. This is the frame the symmetry groups' matrices are written in, so the arrows say which way a group's axes are meant to run: in the z view x points right, y up, and z toward you. They are sized to the selection you have, not to everything the planes could build, so they grow with it. Thickness is adjustable.
Edit planes…
Stellate an arbitrary set of planes instead of a catalog solid — the original program's plane editor. Each row is one plane, written as four numbers (normal, then distance from the origin, full double precision) so it copies and pastes as plain text; then a factor that scales the distance, sliding the plane along its own normal without retyping it; then its own symmetry group — the plane is added together with its whole orbit, whose size is shown beside it. Rows with different groups combine freely: the planes of an icosahedron alongside the planes of a cube, or one solid's planes twice at two depths. A 3D preview draws each plane as a spoke from the origin with a disc lying in it, colored by row. Planes can be imported from a solid, a preset or a file — the same three sources the New dialog offers — each reduced to one row per symmetry class. The sheet saves into the JSON document as before.
