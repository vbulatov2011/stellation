The fifty-nine icosahedra

Twenty planes, extended until they stop crossing, cut space into 473 cells. Choose cells by a few rules about symmetry and connectedness and you get exactly fifty-nine figures — the list Coxeter, Du Val, Flather and Petrie published in 1938. Each card below is a document: click it and the app opens with that arrangement built and those cells chosen.

Reading the symbols

The cells fall into shells, numbered outward from the core and lettered a to h. Three shells hold two kinds of cell and so split in two: e into e₁ and e₂, f into f₁ and f₂, g into g₁ and g₂. A symbol lists exactly the cells the figure is made of, with one abbreviation — a figure that holds a complete shell and everything inside it is named after that outer shell, capitalised, and the inner shells go unwritten. So De₁ means a + b + c + d + e₁, and H means all of them.

One shell has two hands. The 120 cells of f₁ come in mirror-image pairs, and a figure may take both or only one. Taking both leaves the figure reflexible; taking one makes it chiral, and its mirror image is not counted as a separate stellation. That is the whole difference between the two halves of the list: 1–32 are reflexible, and 33–59 are the same constructions with a single hand of f₁ — which is why every one of them has f₁ in its symbol, and why some symbols appear twice. The pictures are coloured by shell, so the letters are visible on the solid: everything of one colour is one shell. Open any card and the cells panel shows the same shells as rows, which is where you can add to a figure or take it apart.
